# Lessons from the Factory Floor: What Fifty Years of Manufacturing Automation Suggests for Enterprise AI

Matt Walker, Acatium, Inc. · 22 October 2026 · As written; this repository tests its argument.

## Key takeaways

- Trust is the constraint on agentic automation, so the opportunity goes to the platform that can show which agents to trust. Joined at the record of each task, ServiceNow's identity, asset, observability, and data capabilities can provide that evidence: which agents performed and how well their work turned out. That evidence lets customers give agents more autonomy over time and gives them a reason to expand their use of ServiceNow.
- The execution layer for enterprise AI is still unclaimed. Claiming it takes three things: a shared language for the work, context that follows the work across systems, and oversight of how it turns out. Because knowledge work shares a common grammar, the market for this layer is likely to consolidate as ERP did rather than split by industry as MES did. ServiceNow is well placed to claim the layer because it already holds much of the system of record.
- The winners will coordinate the work end to end, not automate single tasks. Most AI initiatives are not yet delivering measurable returns because the tools are disconnected from the work around them. The advantage goes to products that define, track, and continuously improve the work across every handoff.
- That requires an agreed definition of the work, and ServiceNow has a head start on writing it. Enterprise AI has protocols for connecting agents but nothing like manufacturing's ISA-95 for the work itself. ServiceNow's ITIL-based definitions are already in daily use across thousands of customers, an advantage that holds if it leads the standard in the open.
- And it requires earning autonomy step by step, not granting it all at once. Manufacturing's most productive plants improved against real results rather than automating everything at once, and people keep using systems that keep proving useful.

## Introduction

In the 1980s, General Motors spent more than $50 billion automating its plants and finished the decade as America's highest-cost carmaker. Over the same years, NUMMI, a Toyota-run joint venture, took over a shuttered GM plant in California, kept much of its workforce, added little new technology, and, within two years, made it the most productive car plant in the country [[1]](https://workofthefuture-taskforce.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/wotf-working-paper-01-2020.pdf). GM automated everything at once. Toyota changed how the work was done and kept what proved itself.

Enterprises show signs of repeating GM's mistake with AI. MIT researchers found that 95% of organizations had seen no measurable return from their generative AI initiatives, and traced the problem to how the tools fit into workflows rather than to model quality [[2]](https://rcpmag.com/Articles/2025/08/25/Only-1-in-20-AI-Investments-Deliver-ROI.aspx). Gartner expects more than 40% of agentic AI projects to be canceled by the end of 2027 and urges companies to focus on enterprise productivity rather than individual task augmentation [[3]](https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027). The shortfall is not in the agents themselves but in how their work connects across systems and teams.

Manufacturing's history shows what closes that gap. Automation's value stalls where work crosses systems and teams, and it moves past that point only when an execution layer coordinates the handoffs and keeps improving them. In enterprise AI, that execution layer is still unclaimed. ServiceNow starts with an advantage the other contenders lack, the record of work for about 8,700 customers [[4]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1373715/000137371526000007/now-20251231.htm), but model labs, Microsoft, and Salesforce are approaching from other directions. Whoever builds the layer first will shape how enterprise AI creates value.

## 1. Automation's gains stalled at the handoffs until an execution layer connected them

Through the 1970s and 1980s factories automated in islands. Material requirements planning (MRP) software planned production, numerically controlled machines and robots ran tasks, and quality and maintenance systems kept their own records. Each island worked, but no one could see or govern production end to end. In the 1980s GM led the attempt to connect everything at once. It spent upwards of $50 billion, yet robots at plants such as Hamtramck often stood idle [[1]](https://workofthefuture-taskforce.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/wotf-working-paper-01-2020.pdf), and the Manufacturing Automation Protocol (MAP), the factory-network standard it championed with Boeing and others, required specialized token-bus networking that proved too complex and expensive, and lost out to cheaper Ethernet and TCP/IP [[5]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manufacturing_Automation_Protocol).

What worked was narrower, and it grew from practice. Through the 1990s, vendors built manufacturing execution systems (MES) plant by plant. An MES sits between business planning and the machines: it dispatches work, checks it, and records how every product was made. In 2000 the International Society of Automation (ISA) published ISA-95, which standardized only the information passing between planning and operations. It codified practice that already worked, and no vendor owned it [[6]](https://www.isa.org/isa-95). At the machine level, OPC, an open interface for reading machine data launched by a group of automation vendors in 1996, played a similar role [[7]](https://jp.opcfoundation.org/?p=198).

The result was a large market that no one owns. Spending on MES now runs at roughly $16–19 billion a year [[8]](https://www.marketsandmarkets.com/Market-Reports/manufacturing-execution-systems-mes-market-536.html), [[9]](https://www.mordorintelligence.com/industry-reports/manufacturing-execution-systems-market), yet the five largest vendors hold only about a quarter to a third of it [[10]](https://www.marketsandmarkets.com/ResearchInsight/manufacturing-execution-systems-mes-market.asp). The reason is physical: factory processes differ so much that specialists won industry by industry [[11]](https://iot-analytics.com/product/mes-market-report-2025-2031/). Enterprise resource planning (ERP) went the other way. Because finance and supply-chain processes look alike across companies, its market is roughly twice as concentrated: the five largest vendors hold about 55% of revenue, and SAP alone 22–24% [[12]](https://erp-software.org/en/erp-market-shares/), [[13]](https://www.appsruntheworld.com/top-100-erp-vendors-in-2012/).

Enterprise AI's execution layer is likely to behave more like ERP than MES. Knowledge work has few physical constraints, and the grammar of tasks, approvals, exceptions, and changes is common across industries, even where regulation and domain vocabulary differ. The prize is therefore larger than it was in manufacturing, and the competition for it sharper.

## 2. Adoption depends on trust, and trust requires an accurate record of the whole task

Enterprise automation has its own islands. Companies are deploying agents one task at a time, inside individual systems: one agent in the IT service desk, another in HR, another in finance. But real work crosses those systems. Onboarding a new employee, for example, runs through HR, IT, and finance in turn. The tools that keep that work safe are islands too. Asset inventories record what exists, identity tools record who and what can reach it, observability tools record what each piece of software did, and data catalogs record what the data means. Each tool sees only its own part of the work. When people did the work, they carried the context from one system to the next. When agents do it, no one does: each agent completes its step, but no single tool records the whole task from request to outcome. Errors also compound: if each step is 95% reliable, ten steps succeed together only about 60% of the time.

The stakes are higher than in a factory. A machine fails in one place, bounded by what it can physically touch; an agent fails at software speed across every system it can access. That makes enterprise AI resemble regulated manufacturing more than manufacturing in general. Pharmaceutical regulators require a complete record of every batch, showing each significant step and who performed or checked it [[14]](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-C/part-211/subpart-J/section-211.188). For agents with access to production systems, credentials, or customer data, a record of the whole task, showing who did what, with which permissions, and with what outcome, is likely to become a condition for companies to adopt them. For high-risk uses it already is: from December 2027, the EU AI Act requires high-risk AI systems to log their activity automatically over their lifetime [[15]](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-12). That record belongs in the system that coordinates the work, the execution layer, and in IT service management (ITSM) the ticket already is that record.

## 3. Two strategies to own the layer

Section 2 points to two gaps: the work that crosses systems has no shared definition, and customers cannot yet see whether agents can be trusted with it. Each gap suggests a strategy. The first closes the definition gap: define the work that passes between systems, independently of the models on which agents run. The second closes the trust gap: compete on earned autonomy, through a loop that proves task by task which agents can be trusted and how well their work turns out.

### Own the vocabulary of work, not the models

The industry is quickly standardizing how agents connect. The Model Context Protocol (MCP) and Agent2Agent (A2A) play the role OPC played for machines, and ServiceNow, Microsoft, and Salesforce all support them [[16]](https://techcrunch.com/?p=3004188), [[17]](https://www.servicenow.com/platform/ai-agent-fabric.html), [[18]](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/mulesoft-agent-fabric-announcement/). Observability is following: OpenTelemetry is becoming the common format for agent traces [[19]](https://www.truefoundry.com/blog/opentelemetry-genai-semantic-conventions), and agent frameworks such as LangChain now give away the framework and charge for tracing and evaluation [[20]](https://blog.langchain.com/series-b). But a trace records what an agent called, not whether the task succeeded. What is missing is what ISA-95 supplied: an agreed meaning for the things that pass between systems (see the appendix). Today's protocols standardize calls and generic task states such as "input-required" or "rejected" [[21]](https://docs.rs/a2a-protocol-types/latest/a2a_protocol_types/task/enum.TaskState.html), but there is no shared way to say a task is an approval waiting on a named owner, an exception against a specific policy, or a change that needs review. Whoever leads that vocabulary in the open makes their execution layer the reference point for everyone else's agents.

ServiceNow has a head start. Its definitions of incidents, requests, changes, and approvals are in daily use across thousands of customers, and they come from ITIL, a framework for IT service management owned by a certification body rather than a software vendor [[22]](https://www.peoplecert.org/news-and-announcements/2021/peoplecert-completes-axelos-acquisition), much as ISA-95 codified practice that MES vendors and their customers had already developed. The history attaches one condition: MAP faded because one company sponsored it, while ISA-95 and OPC succeeded because no vendor owned them. The vocabulary should start where ITIL already defines the objects, in IT service management, and federate business meanings such as customer or risk that each customer owns.

The vocabulary should also stay independent of the models. Manufacturing kept process logic separate from machines: under ISA-88, ISA's batch-control standard, a recipe could move to different equipment without being rewritten [[23]](https://yokogawa.com/library/resources/media-publications/standardizing-batch-control). AI models are today's machines. When process definitions and the record of work live in the execution layer rather than inside a model vendor's platform, a company can switch models, or run open-weight models such as ServiceNow's own Apriel [[24]](https://www.servicenow.com/uk/company/media/press-room/nvidia-enterprise-ai-partnership.html), without exposing how it works. That gives customers a reason to prefer a neutral execution layer over one bundled with a model.

### Compete on earned autonomy, not raw autonomy

Model labs and agent vendors compete on how much an agent can do on its own. ServiceNow can compete on how safely and quickly customers can grant agents autonomy. Manufacturing supports that choice: MacDuffie found the most productive auto plants combined flexible automation with flexible work practices [[1]](https://workofthefuture-taskforce.mit.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/wotf-working-paper-01-2020.pdf), and at NUMMI workers rather than engineers designed their own procedures [[25]](https://www.bookshare.org/browse/book/1331575). The platform does this through a loop: it observes how work is done, learns where agents are reliable, recommends what to hand over next, and measures the result, so customers can extend autonomy step by step, from assisting, to acting with human checkpoints, to running steps whose track record clears an agreed threshold.

Partners carry the organizational half of that bundle. A platform can measure work and recommend changes, but redesigning roles, decision rights, and processes around agents is change management, which consulting and implementation partners already deliver. If each engagement starts from the platform's evidence and feeds its results back into the loop, partners become a channel for the organizational change the strategy depends on.

People stay in that loop. From December 2027, the EU AI Act requires high-risk AI systems to let people oversee and override them [[26]](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-14), [[27]](https://fpf.org/blog/the-ai-act-implementation-timeline-what-changes-under-the-ai-omnibus/), and as AI takes over work, people shift to supervising it, which is harder and can erode their skills [[28]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.11364v1), [29]. The loop should make them better at the work: an AI assistant that spread top performers' practices raised the productivity of more than 5,000 support agents by 14%, and of novices by 34% [[30]](https://www.nber.org/papers/w31161). The loop should also learn how work flows, not why decisions are made, which is customers' know-how; AI that monitors or evaluates workers is itself high-risk under the Act [[31]](https://www.mishcon.com/eu-uk-ai-navigator/annex/no-3).

The loop's output is a trustworthy, real-time view of each task and its outcome, rather than of an agent's run. The platform builds it by joining identity, asset, observability, and data findings at the record of each task. That view puts a platform on the critical path of how a customer improves, a stronger reason to stay than the cost of leaving, and people keep using systems that keep proving useful [[32]](https://aisel.aisnet.org/misq/vol25/iss3/2), [[33]](https://docs.edtechhub.org/lib/W4SUF2B4). ServiceNow is building toward it: AI Control Tower lets administrators approve, monitor and pause agent connections [[34]](https://www.servicenow.com/community/servicenow-otto-articles/action-fabric-mcp-server-mcp-client-and-a2a-explained/ta-p/3557794), and AI Passport, announced for a future release, will define which actions each agent may take [[35]](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20261021300918/en/). The next step is to govern by results, tightening an agent's authority when its work on a task comes out wrong.

The loop can start in IT service management, which already has ITIL's shared vocabulary, measurable outcomes, and a ticket for every unit of work. It need not wait for perfect data: a study of MRP found that how systems were introduced mattered more than data accuracy [[36]](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/soc/wbs/research/ikon/oaklandfurniture/companybackground/mrpbenefitsandcosts), and corrections captured by the loop improve the configuration management database (CMDB) with each turn. From ITSM, the same grammar can extend to HR, customer service, and other functions, and to industries through vocabulary and regulation layered on top, as ServiceNow's industry products already do [[4]](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1373715/000137371526000007/now-20251231.htm). Each extension builds on the evidence of the last, so an early start compounds.

## 4. The layer is contested, and still unclaimed

In manufacturing, the execution layer was contested from both sides. SAP bought its way down from planning, acquiring Lighthammer, Factory Logic, and Visiprise between 2005 and 2008 [[37]](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/08-06-24-sap_to_acquire_visiprise/), [[38]](https://www.controlglobal.com/industrynews/2008/248/), and Rockwell Automation acquired Plex in 2021 to come up from the machines [[39]](https://www.rockwellautomation.com/en-us/company/news/press-releases/Rockwell-Automation-to-Expand-Industrial-Cloud-Software-Offering-with-Acquisition-of-Plex-Systems.html). Neither won outright. The contest in enterprise AI has the same structure. Microsoft is coming from identity with Agent 365 [[40]](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-agent-365/overview), Salesforce from integration with MuleSoft Agent Fabric [[18]](https://www.salesforce.com/news/stories/mulesoft-agent-fabric-announcement/), and the model labs from the models: OpenAI's Frontier gives each agent its own identity, permissions, and guardrails [[41]](https://openai.com/index/introducing-openai-frontier/). ServiceNow comes from the workflow, with what the others lack: the record of the task itself.

Each starting point gives a different view. A model provider sees its model's reasoning, an identity platform sees who an agent is, and an integration platform sees which systems it calls. A workflow platform sees what was asked, who did each step, and how it turned out, which is the view customers need before they grant agents autonomy. That gives ServiceNow the starting point most relevant to adoption, but position alone did not settle manufacturing's contest: buying the pieces built strong positions, not the integrated layer. In a 2026 Rockwell Automation survey, 93% of manufacturers ran an MES somewhere, but only 23% had integrated it fully [[42]](https://www.prnewswire.com/apac/news-releases/93-of-manufacturers-have-mes-but-only-23-have-fully-integrated-it-new-rockwell-automation-report-finds-302824277.html). In enterprise AI, that integrated layer is still unclaimed, and it will go to whoever joins the pieces, not whoever owns the most of them.

## Conclusion: three priorities for ServiceNow

In manufacturing, automation stopped paying off where work crossed systems, until an execution layer grew out of practice and a shared standard defined what passed between planning and the floor. Enterprise AI is reaching the same point: customers are unlikely to hand agents consequential work they cannot see end to end.

The history suggests three priorities, in order. First, in the near term, make the record of each task the place where identity, asset, observability, and data findings join, since that record is what makes autonomy safe to grant. Second, propose an open definition of agentic work, built on ITIL, before a competitor defines the layer around its own view. Third, over the medium term, build learning into the platform: ongoing reviews of agents and the people who work with them, so that people can refine the process and adjust each agent's autonomy, with partners carrying the organizational change the platform cannot deliver alone.

The lesson from GM and Toyota is that automation pays off only when the work around it changes too. The platform that helps enterprises make that change with agents is the one most likely to hold the execution layer.

## Appendix: the ISA-95 model and the enterprise value stream

ISA-95 standardizes only the information that crosses between business planning (level 4) and operations (level 3) [[6]](https://www.isa.org/isa-95). It defines four kinds of information, each with a counterpart in an enterprise value stream [[43]](https://www.symestic.com/en-us/what-is/isa-95).

| ISA-95 category | What it covers in manufacturing | Enterprise value stream equivalent | What agentic work still needs |
|---|---|---|---|
| Product definition | What to make, and how | Process definition: what the work is and its acceptance criteria, independent of the model that runs it | Acceptance criteria against which an agent's output can be checked |
| Production capability | What the plant can do, with which people, equipment, and materials | Which agents and people are permitted and proven for which steps | Evidence of each agent's reliability on each kind of step, not just its permissions |
| Production schedule | What to make, and when | Work requests and assignments: what needs doing, by when, and by whom | Rules for handing work between agents and people, including when to escalate |
| Production performance | What was actually made, and how it went | The task record: what was done, by whom, under which policy, and with what outcome | The outcome of each task, recorded so autonomy can be granted or withdrawn on results |

An agreed definition of these four objects for agentic work is what section 3 calls the vocabulary of work. Nothing comparable exists yet, but ServiceNow already holds parts of all four. Catalog items and workflows define the work. The CMDB, identity governance [[44]](https://www.servicenow.com/products/veza.html), and AI Control Tower [[34]](https://www.servicenow.com/community/servicenow-otto-articles/action-fabric-mcp-server-mcp-client-and-a2a-explained/ta-p/3557794) describe what people and agents can reach and are permitted to do, and AI Passport is set to define each agent's permitted actions [[35]](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20261021300918/en/). Requests and assignments schedule the work. The ticket, joined with agent observability [[45]](https://traceloop.com/blog/traceloop-is-joining-servicenow), records how it turned out.
